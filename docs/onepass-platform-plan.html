The decision to make before any more code

The two directions point at different products, and it is worth naming that plainly before building further.

The product shipped today is hospitality, finance, retail and entertainment: a traveller, merchants, partner analytics. The direction being considered is credentials, campus and verification — students, certificates, visitors, issuers.

These are not the same product. But the gap is smaller than it looks, and the direction of travel matters:

What generalises

A hotel accepting a guest's pass and a campus gate accepting a student's ID are the same operation: a holder presents a short-lived proof, a verifier checks it against an issuer, and consent decides what the verifier gets to see. That engine is already built and tested.

What is genuinely new

A credential as a first-class object — issued by an organization, typed, dated, expiring, revocable, independently verifiable long after the holder has left. The current schema has no such entity. It is an addition, not a rewrite.

Recommendation

Ship hospitality and retail as the product, build the credential platform as the company. Position ONEPASS as a trust layer whose first vertical happens to be hospitality and retail — the same pass, the same consent model, one industry deep. Then aim the go-to-market at campus, where the issuers live. Do not rewrite a working product into a campus product to chase the second market; add the credential entity behind it and let the shipped surface keep earning.

The AI assistant, specifically

Sections 23 and 24 of your brief are the highest-risk features in it. An assistant answering "which credentials expire soon?" must run every query through the same authorization guard as the API — an LLM with database access and no permission check is a data breach with a chat interface. And a document assistant must never render a verdict on authenticity: it can read a date, flag a missing field, and say "this needs issuer verification". Build it in Phase 3, behind the same authorize() call, or not at all.

User flows

Student — onboarding to verified ID

College imports roll→SMS invite→Mobile OTP→Set password→Credential auto-issued→Pass live

The student never fills a form. The registrar's CSV already holds the truth; asking a student to re-type it invites errors and drop-off.

Organization admin — issuing a certificate

Verify org→Define credential type→Upload recipients→Review→Issue batch→Each holder notified

Verifier — an employer checking a degree

Scan QR / enter ID→Public page→VERIFIED · issuer · dates · status

No login. No app. Signed-out and cacheable, because the person checking is a stranger with thirty seconds. The page shows what the credential asserts and who asserts it — never the holder's contact details.

Security officer — a gate

Scan pass→5 checks→Entitlement at this gate→Allow / refuse→Entry logged

Must work with intermittent network: verify signature and expiry locally, queue the entry record, reconcile when the connection returns. A gate that stops when the wifi does will be propped open within a week.

Company HR — visitor management

Visitor pre-registers→OTP→Host approves→Time-boxed QR→Reception scan→Auto-expire on exit

Event organiser

Create event→Registration→Pass issued→Entry scan→Session attendance→Certificate issued from attendance

The last arrow is the product. Attendance data becomes a verifiable credential automatically — that is what an organiser will pay for, not the ticketing.

The credential table, specifically

Three fields decide whether this is a real verification platform:

  • A content hash over the asserted claims, so tampering is detectable independently of the row.
  • An issuer signature — Ed25519 over that hash, with the issuing organization's public key published on its profile. This is what makes a ONEPASS certificate verifiable by a third party who does not trust ONEPASS, only the issuer. It is also the honest answer to "what if you go out of business".
  • A revocation record that is separate from the credential row, so revoking is an append, never an update of history.

Identifiers are ULIDs and public references are separate random strings, so a credential id printed on a certificate reveals neither issue order nor volume.

G

Security architecture

Authentication

Argon2id at 19 MiB / 2 passes. Login always costs one hash whether or not the account exists, so timing cannot enumerate users. Sessions are opaque 256-bit tokens; only their SHA-256 is stored, so a database dump yields nothing usable. "Log out everywhere" bumps one integer on the user row and every session stops resolving.

Authorization

Partner permissions are computed per membership, never globally. authorize() refuses to answer a partner question without an organization id, and hands the handler back the id it must filter on — so forgetting a tenant filter is a type error rather than a breach. Roles, organization ids and permissions are never read from the client.

Verification and QR

The QR carries a reference and a short-lived signed proof — never personal data. Anti-replay is a unique database constraint, so two concurrent scans cannot both win. Long-lived credential QRs carry only an opaque id; everything meaningful comes from the server, gated by consent.

Audit

Append-only, with a sanitiser that strips anything credential-shaped at any depth before it can be written. IP addresses and login identifiers are stored as peppered HMACs — correlatable for investigation, unreadable at rest.

What is not true yet

No penetration test. No third-party audit. No PCI or DPDP assessment. The architecture is designed toward those standards and the code avoids the usual failures, but do not write "bank-grade", "100% secure" or "compliant" anywhere in the product until an assessment exists. Enterprise buyers check.

Why campus, and why not the others

Certificate verification is the better business and the worse first move. It is worth nothing until issuers are on the platform, and issuers are colleges. Campus is how you acquire the supply side while getting paid for it. Sequence, not preference.

Visitor management is a trap. Easy to sell, ₹5k a month, a dozen incumbents, and it teaches you nothing about credentials. It will feel like traction and consume a year.

Campus is also where a first pilot is easiest to place: a single department, running for one term with real users, produces the entry-time and card-replacement numbers that the whole later sales case rests on.

The first twelve months

Months 1–3

One campus, free, instrumented

Your own. One department if that is what you can get. Digital ID + gate + library. Measure entry times and card-replacement costs — those numbers are your entire sales deck later.

Months 4–6

Three paid pilots

₹1–2L each, discounted for the reference and a quotable outcome. Nearby colleges where a principal will take a meeting because a peer institution asked them to.

Months 7–9

Certificates, and the first verifier

Convocation season. Issue verifiable degrees for a graduating batch. Then approach the employers who hire from those colleges — now the verification API has something to verify.

Months 10–12

Repeatable sale

Fixed price, two-week onboarding, self-serve import. Target 12–15 institutions. This is the point at which the thing is a business rather than a project.

The one metric that matters

Verifications per credential per year. Credentials issued is a vanity number — a college will happily issue 5,000 IDs that nobody ever scans. A credential that gets checked is a credential someone depends on, and dependence is what renews a contract.

One, five, ten years

Year 1

A campus product with paying colleges

15–30 institutions, ₹50L–₹1cr ARR, one state. ONEPASS is student ID, gate access and verifiable certificates. Small, real, defensible.

Year 3

The verification API starts to matter

Enough graduates carry ONEPASS credentials that employers begin checking them. Revenue shifts from seats toward verifications — the first sign of a network rather than a tool.

Year 5

Two-sided: issuers and verifiers

Institutions, employers and event organisers on one graph. ₹10–25cr ARR. Enterprise adoption starts, driven by HR teams already verifying candidates. White-label for large universities.

Year 10

Infrastructure, if the network held

Credentials pass between organizations without either integrating with the other, because both speak ONEPASS. Revenue is mostly usage. At that point the interesting question is interoperability with W3C Verifiable Credentials and whatever India's digital-credential standards have become — and the company that implements the standard rather than resisting it is the one that survives it.

The honest version: most of the twenty-two products in your brief will never be built, and that is fine. Companies that become infrastructure do it by being the best in the world at one small thing for long enough that everything else has to route through them. For ONEPASS that thing is: a credential a stranger can trust in three seconds, and revoke in one.
